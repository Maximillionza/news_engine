## Specific questions requiring your opinion

1. **Money-transmitter / payment-facilitation characterization.** Does the invoice → payment-trigger → payslip mechanic, despite the app never holding or moving funds itself, risk being characterized as regulated payment facilitation under South African law?

**It will be low risk, provided Mbucks stays a pure unit-of-account and never becomes transferable or redeemable — but this is a design constraint, not a static fact, and it needs to be locked into the product spec, not just the current build.**

The relevant perimeter is the **National Payment System Act 78 of 1998** (NPS Act), which defines a "payment system" as one that "enables payments to be effected **or facilitates the circulation of money**" (s1). The South African Reserve Bank (SARB) — which oversees the NPS together with the Payments Association of South Africa (PASA) — has in a recent joint communication interpreted "payment" narrowly, distinguishing systems that actually move or circulate money from ancillary information/calculation layers. [Likely] MiniMoney's invoice → payslip mechanic is closer to the latter: it calculates and displays an amount owed, but the actual transfer of value happens entirely outside the app, on the parent's own banking rails, which the app has no visibility into or control over. That is structurally different from, say, an e-wallet or a peer-to-peer transfer app.

Two things would change this analysis, and both are worth building explicit guardrails against:

**E-money issuance.** SARB's Position Paper on e-money restricts the issuance of e-money to registered banks. [Certain] As long as Mbucks cannot be spent, transferred between users, or redeemed for value anywhere other than through the parent's independent decision to pay via their own banking app, Mbucks functions as a scorekeeping unit, not e-money. If a future roadmap item lets a child "spend" Mbucks in-app, at a merchant, or transfer them to another user, that is a different regulatory animal and this analysis would need to be redone.

**Form vs substance.** South African courts do not treat labels as decisive of legal character. In *Maize Board v Jackson* 2005 (6) SA 592 (SCA), the SCA held that parties "may not call a contract by a name or give it a shape intended not to express, but to disguise, its true nature" — a court will look at the substance, not the label. [Certain as to the principle; Likely as to application here] This cuts both ways for you: it means the "invoice/payslip" terminology doesn't *by itself* create payment-facilitation risk (form doesn't control), but it also means you can't rely on "we just call it an invoice, we don't touch the money" as a complete answer if the substance ever does start to resemble fund-holding or transmission — for example, if a future version of the account-linking feature moved from read-only to write access, or if Mbucks acquired any transfer/redemption function. This is a genuine point of ongoing legal exposure, not something the current build closes off permanently.

The **NCR's Payment Distribution Agent (PDA)** category — the one financial-facilitator role the business's own research correctly flagged as a candidate — is anchored specifically to debt review: NCA s44A defines a PDA as a person who "distributes payments to credit providers... in terms of a debt rearrangement" under debt counselling. [Certain] There is no credit agreement, credit provider, or debt counsellor anywhere in the MiniMoney fact pattern, so PDA registration does not appear to be triggered. That said, PDA is a category *within* the NCA; it doesn't tell you anything about NPS Act exposure, which is the more directly relevant regime and is currently in flux (see Q6).

2. **POPIA Section 34/35 sufficiency of the consent model.** The app now uses a single, universal rule: no minor may access any part of the app without a pre-existing, consenting parent account (no carve-outs of any kind — an earlier design allowing limited pre-consent minor registration was removed specifically because of this open question). Is this consent model, as described, sufficient under POPIA's competent-person consent requirements?

**The universal parent-consent gate satisfies the letter of s35(1)(a) — but "satisfies the letter" and "will survive Information Regulator scrutiny of how consent was actually obtained" are two different questions, and only the second one is where real risk lives.**

POPIA s34 prohibits processing a child's personal information unless one of the s35 exceptions applies; s35(1)(a) is "the prior consent of a competent person." [Certain] A "competent person" is defined as someone with parental responsibilities under the Children's Act 38 of 2005 — a parent or legal guardian. [Certain] By removing the earlier design that allowed limited pre-consent minor registration, and requiring a pre-existing consenting parent account before a minor accesses *any* part of the app, MiniMoney has structurally satisfied the s35(1)(a) gateway more cleanly than the median South African app aimed at minors, most of which rely on the (legally shaky) practice of a clickwrap age-gate that a child can simply lie through. [Likely]

Where I'd push you to go further than "we have a checkbox":

**Burden of proof sits with you.** POPIA s11(2)(a) places the burden of proving valid consent on the responsible party. [Certain] "Valid" consent under POPIA's s1 definition must be "voluntary, specific and informed" — a generic account-creation flow that doesn't clearly separate what the parent is consenting to (data processing) from what they're agreeing to (T&Cs generally, or the subscription) is a common failure mode that regulators and plaintiffs' attorneys look for first.

**No robust age/identity verification is legally required by POPIA itself**, but the absence of any verification mechanism has been specifically flagged in South African legal commentary as weakening reliance on a "just click here to confirm you're the parent" flow — a child could plausibly create the "parent" account itself. [Likely] This doesn't invalidate the model, but a lightweight verification step (even something as unglamorous as a card micro-authorization or matching parent ID details to an existing bank-linked identity) would materially de-risk this specific question, and given the read-only bank-linking feature is already planned, there may be a natural integration point here worth flagging to product.

I'd characterize this as: **the model is legally sufficient as designed**

3. **POPIA Section 14 retention sufficiency.** Our own research found POPIA's general data-retention principle (information not retained longer than necessary; correction/deletion on request within 30 days) but found no minor-specific supplementary retention rule (comparable to COPPA or GDPR-K) within POPIA itself. Is the general principle sufficient for this specific data model, or is additional design needed?

**Short answer: POPIA has no minor-specific retention supplement — but "no supplement exists" doesn't mean "the general principle is undemanding"; it's arguably the opposite, and it will require you to actually design the deletion mechanics, not just cite the section.**

POPIA's Condition 5 (s14) requires that records not be kept longer than necessary for the purpose for which they were collected, subject to the data subject's (or competent person's) right to request correction or deletion. [Certain] There is no COPPA-style "delete by default at age X" or GDPR-K equivalent bright-line rule in POPIA. That is a real, confirmed gap, not something the desk research missed.

The practical consequence is that the general s14 "necessity" test has to be applied with unusual rigor for MiniMoney precisely *because* there's no minor-specific floor to fall back on:

What is the retention *purpose* for a child's task-completion, payslip, and (if built) transaction-verification history, and does that purpose expire at some point — e.g., when the child ages out of the platform, or when the parent's subscription lapses? [Guessing — this is a product-policy question dressed as a legal one; POPIA gives you the test, not the answer]

The Information Regulator has enforcement powers under POPIA Chapter 10 (ss73–99), and given children's data carries the heightened protection of ss34/35, a regulator reviewing a complaint about this app would likely scrutinize retention *harder*, not more leniently, precisely because there's no statutory floor telling them what's "acceptable" — meaning the burden is on you to justify a specific retention period and deletion trigger in your own privacy documentation, rather than relying on POPIA's silence as permission to keep data indefinitely. [Likely]

4. **Minor contractual capacity.** The payment obligation is structured to run against the parent, not the child — the child is a participant who is remunerated on task completion, not a party bound by any enforceable obligation. Our own (non-legal) research found no South African law or act that appears to bar this specific parent-child arrangement, but this has not been confirmed by counsel. Does this structure hold up, and does the "domestic agreement" presumption (that family arrangements are not intended to be legally binding) affect how the payslip/invoice mechanic should be characterized or documented?

**this is the strongest of your seven positions, and there's a specific, on-point common-law doctrine that supports it better than the brief's own framing suggests.**

South African common law and the Children's Act 38 of 2005 (s17) fix majority at 18; children between 7 and 18 have *limited* contractual capacity, curable by parental/guardian assistance, while a contract concluded by an unassisted minor is not void but "unilaterally enforceable" against the minor — sometimes called a "limping contract" — meaning the minor's parent/guardian can elect to ratify or repudiate it, while the other party remains bound regardless. [Certain, supported by *Van Dyk v South African Railways and Harbours* 1956 (4) SA 410 (W); *Motors WP (Pty) Ltd v Swart* 1976 (3) SA 221 (C); *Ten Brink NO v Motala* 2001 (1) SA 1011 (D)]

But there's a cleaner doctrine that fits your facts even better than "minor with limited capacity assisted by guardian," and it's one the business's own research didn't surface: **a minor may enter into a contract *without* parental assistance at all, if the contract imposes rights but no obligations on the minor** — i.e., it is purely to the minor's benefit. The classic examples are accepting a donation or an inheritance. MiniMoney's structure — child performs tasks, child receives money the parent already owes, child bears no enforceable payment obligation to anyone — fits this exception more naturally than it fits the "assisted contract" framework, because there may be no contract requiring the child's capacity at all in the relevant legal sense. The child is closer to a beneficiary of a parental undertaking than a contracting party.

On the "domestic agreement" framing: South African law does not import England's *Balfour v Balfour* line wholesale, but it reaches a similar place through its own doctrine of **animus contrahendi** — a contract requires serious intention by the parties to create a legally enforceable obligation, tested objectively and subjectively (*Pitout v North Cape Livestock Co-operative Control Ltd* 1977 (4) SA 842 (A); the foundational case establishing animus contrahendi as the touchstone of SA contract formation is *Conradie v Rossouw* [Guessing on the precise law-report citation — I have not independently verified the pinpoint reference and it should be checked before citing in any filed document]). The practical upshot: whether the parent-child arrangement is "legally binding" in the *conventional* creditor/debtor sense is somewhat beside the point, because the structure doesn't need it to be. The parent's obligation to pay is arguably better characterized as a unilateral undertaking or conditional donation than a bilateral contract at all — which sidesteps the capacity question rather than resolving it in the child's favour, and is, if anything, a more defensible position than treating this as a binding contract with a family-arrangement carve-out.

5. **Terminology risk.** Our research confirmed **no existing ARB ruling or National Credit Regulator guidance addresses this exact question** — this is a genuine gap in precedent, not something we simply couldn't find. Does applying employment/consumer-debt terminology ("payslip," "invoice," "late penalty," "arrears") to a product directed at children as young as 6 carry any regulatory or advertising-standards risk under the ARB's Code of Advertising Practice (Clause 14, which prohibits exploiting children's credulity or inexperience) or elsewhere?

**Short answer: This is a real risk, and it's larger under the ARB Code's financial-advertising rules than under the children's-advertising rules the brief focused on.**

Two separate ARB Code of Advertising Practice provisions are engaged, and the business's own research only found one of them:

**Clause 14.2 (Section II — children generally):** "Advertisements should not exploit the natural credulity of children or their lack of experience... and should not strain their sense of loyalty." Clause 14 also states that advertising "aimed at, featuring or likely to influence children" is interpreted **narrowly**, because children take literal meaning more readily than adults. Using employment/consumer-debt vocabulary ("payslip," "arrears," "late penalty") toward a 6-year-old is squarely the kind of literalism concern this clause is designed to catch — a child that age has no schema for "arrears" beyond whatever emotional weight the interface gives it.

**Clause 6.1, Section III (financial products specifically) — not previously identified in your research:** advertisers of financial products must "ensure that consumers are fully aware of the nature of any financial commitments they may be making" and must not "wittingly or unwittingly take advantage of consumers' lack of experience or knowledge of the complexities of financial products." This clause is written for adult financial consumers, but MiniMoney's *entire premise* is presenting financial-product vocabulary to consumers (children) who are definitionally in the position this clause exists to protect against. There is no reported ARB ruling on this exact fact pattern — your research was right that the precedent gap is real — but the absence of a ruling doesn't mean the clause doesn't apply; it means no one has tested it yet, and MiniMoney would be a natural first test case if a competitor, parent advocacy group, or the ARB itself decided to look.

The Consumer Protection Act 68 of 2008 adds a second, non-ARB layer: s3 recognizes minors as a vulnerable consumer category, and ss29/41 prohibit misleading or deceptive marketing practices generally. This doesn't map cleanly onto "payslip" terminology (nobody is being deceived about a product's characteristics), but it's the kind of provision a regulator or plaintiff's lawyer reaches for as a second string to the bow if the ARB angle doesn't land.

**Practical read:** I would not tell the business this terminology is prohibited — nothing found says that — but I would flag it as the single highest-optics-risk item in the whole brief, because it is the one place where the product's own marketing language does the incriminating work for a regulator, with no need to prove anything about the underlying mechanics. A relatively cheap mitigation (renaming child-facing surfaces to less debt-coded language, reserving "invoice"/"arrears" terminology for the parent-facing side only) would meaningfully reduce exposure without touching the underlying architecture.

6. **NCR scope on optional account-linking.** Our own research found that NCR registration is anchored to four defined categories (credit providers, credit bureaus, debt counsellors, Payment Distribution Agents), none of which appears to plausibly cover a read-only, non-custodial bank-account-verification integration (e.g. Stitch/Mono-style). Can you confirm whether offering this as an *optional* parent-controlled feature (never mandatory) would trigger NCR or any other South African payment-services regulatory obligation?

**Short answer: not an NCR PDA issue;**

Your research correctly found that NCR registration is anchored to four categories — credit providers, credit bureaus, debt counsellors, and PDAs (NCA s40 read with s44A) — none of which plausibly covers a read-only, non-custodial account-verification integration. [Certain] A read-only Stitch/Mono-style integration doesn't distribute payments, extend credit, or counsel debtors; it retrieves data. This part of the question has a clean answer and I'd treat it as resolved.

Where I'd push back on treating this as fully closed: **NCR is not the only regulator with a payments perimeter**, and this is the one place your research may have stopped one level too early. The SARB — via the National Payment System Act and its oversight of PASA — regulates "third-party payment providers" as a distinct category from banks, and SARB issued a **Draft Directive in early 2025** specifically opening NPS access to fintechs, alongside a **Draft Exemption Notice under the Banks Act** clarifying which non-bank payment-related activities can operate without a banking licence. [Certain that these drafts exist and were in circulation in 2025; Guessing as to their final, in-force form as of today, since I have not been able to verify whether they've been finalized] Whether a *read-only, parent-opt-in, non-custodial* account-info feature falls inside or outside that perimeter is genuinely unsettled — open banking / account-information-service regulation in South Africa is still developing, and I found no authority squarely on point either way. This is a real gap, not one your research vendor should have been expected to close, but it means "optional and read-only" is a good starting position, not a guarantee, and the answer could firm up (in either direction) as SARB's fintech-access framework is finalized. I'd recommend flagging this specific feature for a compliance re-check closer to build time rather than treating today's answer as durable.

7. **App-store child-category cross-check.** We've identified that Google Play's Families Policy and Apple's Kids Category guidelines both impose disclosure/design requirements on the app's non-monetary rewards system (Mpoints) independent of any real-money transaction. Is there any South African-specific regulatory overlay on top of these platform policies we should be aware of?

**Short answer: There is a South Africa-specific overlay, and it's not the one the brief was looking toward — it's the Film and Publication Board, and it may apply to the Mpoints reward mechanic specifically because it has game-like, interactive characteristics.**

Google Play's Families Policy and Apple's Kids Category are platform contract terms, not law — useful for app-store approval, irrelevant to South African statutory exposure. The layer your research didn't surface: the **Films and Publications Act 65 of 1996**, as amended by the **Films and Publications Amendment Act 11 of 2019** (in force from 1 March 2023), gives the Film and Publication Board (FPB) a classification mandate over "interactive computer games" and — post-amendment — certain online/commercial digital distribution more broadly. [Certain as to the Act and amendment; Likely, not Certain, as to whether MiniMoney's specific reward mechanic would be caught]

Whether Mpoints and its associated gamified elements (badges, cosmetic rewards, task-completion mechanics) constitute an "interactive computer game" for FPB purposes, or fall under the Act's broader "certain publications" language extended to online content, is genuinely fact-specific and turns on features not fully described in the brief (how game-like the UI/UX actually is, whether it has narrative or competitive elements, etc.). [Guessing] What I can say with more confidence: this is a live regulatory regime with actual registration and self-classification-accreditation requirements for commercial online distributors targeting South African audiences, it is specifically child-protection-oriented, and it sits completely outside the POPIA/NCR/ARB frameworks your research vendor was working within — meaning it's very plausible this got missed not through carelessness but because it's a genuinely different silo (content classification, not financial or data-protection regulation).
