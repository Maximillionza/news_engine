## Executive Summary

**Status:** Partial | **Confidence:** Medium | **Evidence:** Supported

TradeTrust is a proposed two-sided marketplace for South Africa's home
improvement and trade services sector (bricklayers, electricians,
plumbers, architects, engineers), intended to solve two linked problems
at once: household customers cannot reliably identify trustworthy
vendors and have no financial protection if delivered work is
unsatisfactory, while vendors carry payment risk (clients withholding
payment for delivered, contracted work), lack a portable cross-platform
credibility record, and have no protection against being "held hostage"
by difficult clients after delivery. The concept is well-articulated on
the problem side — both the customer and vendor pain points are
specifically named, and the target segments (individual/household
customers commissioning single-trade jobs; independent or small-team
artisans in the informal-to-semi-formal trades market) are clearly
stated. However, the submission is silent on nearly every mechanism
question that determines whether the concept is buildable and legal as
described: how "financial protection" would actually work (escrow?
guarantee? insurance? dispute arbitration without holding funds?), what
the business model or monetization is, and how a "credibility signal"
would be verified — a materially different task for a bricklayer than
for a statutorily registered architect or engineer. The evidence base is
explicitly self-flagged by the submitter as anecdotal (a small sample of
friends/colleagues) and not market-validated. This Business Case
surfaces these gaps explicitly rather than assuming they are solved.

## Problem

**Status:** Partial | **Confidence:** Medium | **Evidence:** Supported

Two linked but distinct problems are stated directly in the source:

**Customer-side:** individual/household customers seeking home
improvement and trade services (bricklayers, electricians, plumbers,
architects, engineers) struggle to locate vendors they can trust to
deliver on quality, cost, and timeline. Current practice relies on
informal referral networks with no standardized credibility signal and
no financial protection if work is unsatisfactory.

**Vendor-side:** vendors carry payment risk (clients withholding payment
despite contracted work being delivered), have no portable,
cross-platform credibility record, and have no protection against being
"held hostage" by difficult clients post-delivery.

Both statements are explicit in the case study, which is a genuine
strength relative to a single-sided problem framing — the submission
names the pain on both sides of the intended marketplace rather than
assuming vendor supply will simply follow customer demand. However, the
only cited evidence is "a small sample of friends/colleagues reporting
difficulty locating reliable vendors," and the submitter has explicitly
self-tagged this as "[Anecdotal — not market-validated]." No sample size,
no vendor-side evidence (the vendor pain points appear to be stated
without a cited source at all — only the customer-side problem is
explicitly evidenced), no frequency/severity data, and no independent
corroboration exists. The problem statement is credible on its face but
unvalidated.

## Opportunity

**Status:** Partial | **Confidence:** Low | **Evidence:** Assumed

If the stated problem is real at scale, TradeTrust's differentiator
would be addressing both sides of the transaction simultaneously — a
credibility/reputation layer plus some form of financial protection —
rather than solving only discovery (as a listings/classifieds site does)
or only reputation (as a review site does). This is an inference about
positioning, not a claim made in the source. South Africa's home
improvement and trades sector is known (Incubator general knowledge, not
case-study-sourced) to have a large informal-to-semi-formal segment,
which plausibly makes credibility signaling and payment-risk mitigation
a genuine structural gap rather than a solved problem — but no market
sizing, competitor analysis, or demand validation (e.g. waitlist
signups, vendor or customer surveys) is present in the case study to
confirm this. The two-sided nature of the opportunity also means it
carries the well-known "cold start" risk of two-sided marketplaces
(neither side has value without the other already present), which is not
addressed in the source and is treated separately under Risks.

## Value Proposition

**Status:** Partial | **Confidence:** Medium | **Evidence:** Supported

For customers: a standardized credibility signal (replacing informal,
non-portable referral-network trust) combined with financial protection
if delivered work is unsatisfactory, reducing the risk of engaging an
unknown vendor. For vendors: protection against payment withholding for
delivered, contracted work; a portable, cross-platform credibility
record that survives beyond any single client relationship or referral
network; and protection against being "held hostage" by a difficult
client post-delivery (e.g. a client who delays or refuses payment while
disputing satisfaction after work is complete). This is the clearest,
most concrete part of the original submission — both sides' value
propositions are specific and map directly to the stated problems.
What is not addressed: the actual mechanism behind "financial
protection" (this is the single largest undefined term in the entire
case — see Legal & Compliance and the Payments & Escrow Regulatory
Compliance extension), how credibility is verified or scored, and why a
vendor or customer would trust TradeTrust's guarantee over simply
continuing to use free, socially-embedded referral networks.

## Legal & Compliance

**Status:** Incomplete | **Confidence:** Low | **Evidence:** Unknown

**This section is flagged Critical.** Not addressed at all in the case
study, despite being central to feasibility. Open items include: (1)
whether TradeTrust's "financial protection if work unsatisfactory"
and vendor "protection against payment withholding" mechanisms require
TradeTrust (or a partner) to hold or transmit client funds, which would
implicate South Africa's National Payment System Act 78 of 1998 and
South African Reserve Bank/PASA oversight, and potentially require
partnering with a licensed payments provider or trust-account structure
rather than TradeTrust holding funds directly; (2) whether a "guarantee"
of satisfactory work constitutes a regulated insurance-like product
under the Insurance Act, requiring FSCA authorization, depending on how
the protection is structured; (3) general data protection (POPIA)
obligations for customer and vendor personal information (identity
documents for verification, addresses, payment details), which apply
regardless of the payment-mechanism question; (4) consumer protection
obligations under the Consumer Protection Act relevant to a dispute-
adjudication/refund-like mechanism; and (5) potential liability exposure
if TradeTrust represents a vendor as "verified" or "credible" and that
representation later proves false — particularly acute for the
statutorily regulated trades named in the source (see the Vendor
Verification & Professional Licensing extension). None of this is
addressed in the source submission, and the case study's central value
proposition (financial protection) cannot be assessed as buildable or
legal without resolving item (1) specifically.

## Risks

**Status:** Partial | **Confidence:** Medium | **Evidence:** Supported

Risks identifiable directly from the described mechanic:
- **Regulatory risk (Critical):** a platform offering "financial
  protection" between two transacting parties may require payments or
  insurance-related licensing (or a licensed partner) depending on
  exact mechanism and jurisdiction; see Legal & Compliance.
- **Two-sided cold-start risk:** the platform requires sufficient vendor
  supply and customer demand simultaneously to be useful to either side;
  the case study does not address a sequencing strategy (e.g. seed
  vendors first, or seed demand first) for initial liquidity.
- **Professional-liability/verification risk:** the named vendor
  categories span both informal artisans (bricklayers) and statutorily
  regulated professionals (architects, engineers, and — depending on
  scope of work — electricians and plumbers subject to certification
  regimes). A single undifferentiated "credibility signal" risks either
  under-verifying regulated professionals (liability exposure if a
  falsely "verified" vendor causes harm) or over-engineering verification
  for informal artisans where no formal registration exists to check
  against.
- **Trust/adoption risk:** the case study's own evidence is anecdotal
  (a small sample of friends/colleagues); whether customers and vendors
  will trust a new platform's guarantee over existing free,
  socially-embedded referral networks is unvalidated.
- **Cash/informality risk:** the target market is explicitly described
  as "informal-to-semi-formal," where cash payment outside any platform
  is plausibly common; if transactions can easily happen off-platform
  once a match is made, TradeTrust's ability to enforce its financial-
  protection mechanism (and collect any transaction-based revenue) is at
  risk — this is an Incubator inference from the stated target market,
  not addressed in the source.
- **Competitive risk:** general classifieds and review platforms already
  operate in South Africa (Incubator general knowledge, not sourced from
  case study); TradeTrust's differentiation and defensibility against
  these, and against any existing local trades-marketplace attempt, is
  unaddressed.
- **Evidence-validation risk:** the entire case rests on a small,
  unquantified anecdotal sample explicitly self-flagged by the submitter
  as not market-validated.
