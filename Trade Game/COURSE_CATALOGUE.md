### Lesson 1.1: Trading Terms Every Beginner Needs to Know

**A shorthand worth learning**  
Every new field has its own vocabulary, and trading is no different. This lesson covers the handful of plain-language terms you'll see again and again — learn them once here, in one place, so nothing later on has to stop and explain itself.  
*[Illustration: `term-intro.svg`]*

**Pip**  
A pip is the smallest standard unit price is usually measured in — on most currency pairs, the 4th decimal place. If EUR/USD moves from 1.1000 to 1.1010, it moved 10 pips. It's the 'inch' or 'centimetre' of price movement.  
*[Illustration: `term-pip.svg`]*

**Bid, Ask & Spread**  
The Bid is the price you can sell at right now. The Ask is the price you can buy at right now. The Ask is always slightly higher — that small gap is the spread, a built-in cost on every trade, paid to whoever is making the market.  
*[Illustration: `term-bidask.svg`]*

**Long vs Short**  
Going long means buying first, expecting the price to rise so you can sell it later for more. Going short means selling first, expecting the price to fall so you can buy it back later for less. Both directions are always available.  
*[Illustration: `term-longshort.svg`]*

   - — A trading fee charged by brokers

   - *Explanation:* A pip is the standard smallest unit of price movement — on most pairs, the 4th decimal place.

2. What is the spread?
   - — A bonus paid to winning traders
   - **✓** The gap between the Bid (sell) price and the Ask (buy) price
   - — Another name for leverage

3. You expect a currency pair's price to fall and want to profit from that. What do you do?
   - — Go long (buy first)

   - — Increase your leverage
   - *Explanation:* Going short means selling first, expecting to buy it back later at a lower price — the way to profit from an expected decline.

### Lesson 1.2: What Is a Financial Market

**Markets, in one sentence**  
A financial market is a place where people agree on a price to exchange something — a currency, a company's shares, a commodity like gold — either right now or for future delivery.  
*[Illustration: `market-overview.svg`]*

**The four markets in TradeWise**  
Forex (currency pairs like EUR/USD), Metals (gold, silver), Crypto (BTC, ETH), and Stocks (company shares). TradeWise starts you on Forex — EUR/USD specifically — because it's deep, liquid, and its chart behaviour is the clearest to learn confluence from.  
*[Illustration: `four-markets.svg`]*

**Who's on the other side of your trade**  
Banks, hedge funds, corporations hedging currency exposure, and other retail traders. You are never trading against an abstract 'market' — every trade has a real counterparty taking the opposite side.  
*[Illustration: `participants.svg`]*

**Why price actually moves**  
Simplified supply and demand: more buyers than sellers at a price pushes it up, more sellers than buyers pushes it down. Everything else you learn in this app is about reading the visible footprints of that imbalance on a chart.  
*[Illustration: `supply-demand.svg`]*

### Lesson 1.3: What Is a Candle

**What one candle represents**  
A single candlestick shows four numbers for a fixed time window: Open, High, Low, Close (OHLC). The 'body' spans open-to-close; the thin 'wicks' show the high and low reached during that window.  
*[Illustration: `candle-ohlc.svg`]*

**Bullish vs bearish**  
A bullish (typically green) candle closes higher than it opened — buyers won that window. A bearish (typically red) candle closes lower than it opened — sellers won.  
*[Illustration: `candle-bull-bear.svg`]*

**What a long wick tells you**  
A long wick means price reached further in that direction but got rejected and closed back the other way — a visible sign that side lost control before the window closed.  
*[Illustration: `candle-wick.svg`]*

**End-of-lesson quiz** (2/3 to pass, unlimited retries):

1. What does the 'body' of a candlestick represent?
   - — The highest and lowest price reached
   - **✓** The range between the open and close price
   - — The trading volume
   - — The next candle's likely direction
   - *Explanation:* The body spans from the open price to the close price. The wicks (thin lines) show the high and low reached during the window.

2. A candle opens at 1.1000 and closes at 1.0950. What is it?
   - — Bullish
   - **✓** Bearish
   - — Neutral
   - — A doji
   - *Explanation:* It closed lower than it opened, so sellers won that window — that's a bearish candle.

3. A candle has a long upper wick and a small body near the bottom. What does that suggest?
   - — Buyers pushed price up and held it there
   - **✓** Price pushed higher but was rejected and closed back down
   - — The candle has no meaningful information
   - — Volume was zero
   - *Explanation:* A long upper wick with the close near the low means buyers tried to push price up during the window but were rejected — sellers regained control before the close.

### Lesson 1.4: Types of Candles

**Marubozu — full conviction**  
A long body with almost no wick on either end. Open and close sit right at the extremes — one side controlled the entire window, start to finish, with no meaningful pushback.  
*[Illustration: `candle-marubozu.svg`]*

**Doji — pure indecision**  
Open and close are nearly identical, even though price may have moved noticeably in both directions during the window. Neither buyers nor sellers won — it's a dead heat.  
*[Illustration: `candle-doji-example.svg`]*

**Spinning Top — a tug of war**  
A small body with long wicks on both sides. Price pushed meaningfully higher and lower before settling near where it started — real disagreement, no clear winner.  
*[Illustration: `candle-spinningtop.svg`]*

**End-of-lesson quiz** (2/3 to pass, unlimited retries):

1. What defines a Marubozu candle?
   - — A tiny body with long wicks both sides
   - **✓** A long body with almost no wick on either end
   - — Open and close are nearly equal
   - — It only appears on the 1H timeframe
   - *Explanation:* A Marubozu has almost no wick — the body runs nearly the full high-to-low range, showing one side in complete control.

2. What does a Doji tell you?
   - — One side was in total control
   - **✓** Neither side won — open and close are nearly equal despite price moving both ways
   - — The market is closed
   - — Price broke a key level
   - *Explanation:* A Doji shows a dead heat — open and close land almost exactly together, even if price wandered during the window.

3. A candle has a small body with long wicks on both the top and bottom. What is it?
   - — Marubozu
   - — Doji
   - **✓** Spinning Top
   - — Bullish engulfing
   - *Explanation:* That's a Spinning Top — real pushes in both directions, but neither side held on by the close.

### Lesson 1.5: Candle Formations

**One candle is a hint, not a story**  
A chart is a sequence of candles read left to right. Any single candle's shape only becomes meaningful once you know what came before it — the same shape can mean something different depending on context.  
*[Illustration: `candle-sequence.svg`]*

**What turns a shape into a formation**  
A formation is a specific candle shape (or small group of candles) appearing in a specific place — usually after a clear prior trend. A hammer shape after a decline is a formation; the exact same shape in the middle of a flat range isn't telling you much.  
*[Illustration: `candle-wick.svg`]*

**Single-candle formations vs multi-candle formations**  
Some formations are a single candle read against the prior trend (like a hammer or shooting star). Others need two or three candles working together (like an engulfing pattern or a star pattern). Both types are covered next.  
*[Illustration: `candle-marubozu.svg`]*

**End-of-lesson quiz** (2/3 to pass, unlimited retries):

   - — It doesn't — shape alone always means the same thing
   - **✓** A formation depends on context, especially the trend leading into it
   - — Only the most recent candle ever matters
   - — Candle shapes are decorative and carry no information
   - *Explanation:* Context is what turns a shape into a signal — the same hammer shape means something different after a decline than in the middle of a flat range.

2. What is a formation, as distinct from a single candle shape?
   - — Any random candle on the chart

   - — A candle with high trading volume
   - — A candle that appears on the Daily timeframe only
   - *Explanation:* A formation is a shape plus context — most commonly, that shape appearing right after a clear trend has been established.

### Lesson 1.6: Most Common Candle Patterns

**Bullish Engulfing**  
After a decline, a bearish candle is immediately followed by a larger bullish candle whose body fully swallows the prior one. It signals buyers have decisively taken over.  
*[Illustration: `pattern-bullish-engulfing.svg`]*

**Bearish Engulfing**  
The mirror image: after an advance, a bullish candle is immediately followed by a larger bearish candle whose body fully swallows it. Sellers have decisively taken over.  
*[Illustration: `pattern-bearish-engulfing.svg`]*

**Hammer**  
After a decline, a candle with a small body and a long lower wick — price pushed well below, then buyers rejected the lower prices and closed back up near the open.  
*[Illustration: `pattern-hammer.svg`]*

**Shooting Star**  
After an advance, a candle with a small body and a long upper wick — price pushed well above, then sellers rejected the higher prices and closed back down near the open.  
*[Illustration: `pattern-shootingstar.svg`]*

**Morning Star**  
A 3-candle reversal after a decline: a big bearish candle, a small indecisive candle, then a big bullish candle — the market's mood visibly turning from selling to buying.  
*[Illustration: `pattern-morningstar.svg`]*

**Evening Star**  
The mirror image after an advance: a big bullish candle, a small indecisive candle, then a big bearish candle — the mood turning from buying to selling.  
*[Illustration: `pattern-eveningstar.svg`]*

**End-of-lesson quiz** (2/3 to pass, unlimited retries):

   - — Bearish Engulfing
   - **✓** Bullish Engulfing
   - — Shooting Star
   - — Evening Star
   - *Explanation:* That's a Bullish Engulfing pattern — the larger bullish body fully swallowing the prior bearish body after a decline.

2. A candle after a decline has a small body and a long lower wick. What is it?
   - — Shooting Star
   - **✓** Hammer
   - — Bearish Engulfing
   - — Marubozu
   - *Explanation:* A Hammer — small body, long lower wick, appearing after a decline, signals a rejection of lower prices.

3. Big bearish candle → small indecisive candle → big bullish candle, after a decline. What pattern is this?
   - — Evening Star
   - **✓** Morning Star
   - — Bearish Engulfing
   - — Spinning Top
   - *Explanation:* That three-candle sequence — bearish, indecisive, bullish — after a decline is a Morning Star, one of the clearest reversal signals covered here.

### Lesson 1.7: Timeframes & Chart Navigation

**What a timeframe is**  
Each timeframe (5M, 15M, 1H, 4H, D1) sets how much time one candle covers. A 5-minute (5M) candle covers 5 minutes of trading; a Daily (D1) candle covers a full day.  
*[Illustration: `timeframes-grid.svg`]*

**Zooming out for context, zooming in for precision**  
Higher timeframes (1H, 4H) show the bigger trend and the key levels that matter. Lower timeframes (5M) show the precise moment to act. TradeWise's core mechanic uses 5M for decisions, with 1H available as context.  
*[Illustration: `timeframe-context.svg`]*

**Why TradeWise pauses before you decide**  
The chart pauses at a 5M decision point before you commit — not after price has already moved. This is the single most important habit this app is built to train: deciding under uncertainty, not labelling in hindsight.  
*[Illustration: `pre-hoc.svg`]*

### Lesson 1.8: Trends & Ranges

**Uptrend**  
A series of higher highs and higher lows. Each pullback finds a floor above the last one — buyers are progressively more willing to pay more.  
*[Illustration: `structure-uptrend.svg`]*

**Downtrend**  
A series of lower highs and lower lows — the mirror image. Each rally fails below the last one.  
*[Illustration: `structure-downtrend.svg`]*

**Range**  
Price oscillates between a repeated ceiling and floor with no clear higher-highs/higher-lows or lower-highs/lower-lows progression. Neither side is in control yet.  
*[Illustration: `structure-range.svg`]*

**End-of-lesson quiz** (2/3 to pass, unlimited retries):

1. What defines an uptrend?
   - — Equal highs and equal lows
   - **✓** A series of higher highs and higher lows
   - — Any green candle
   - — Price moving sideways
   - *Explanation:* An uptrend is specifically a pattern of higher highs and higher lows — each pullback holding above the prior low.

2. What defines a downtrend?
   - — A series of higher highs and higher lows
   - **✓** A series of lower highs and lower lows
   - — Price bouncing in a flat range
   - — Any red candle
   - *Explanation:* A downtrend is a series of lower highs and lower lows — the mirror image of an uptrend, with each rally failing below the last one.

3. Price is bouncing between the same ceiling and floor with no clear higher-highs/higher-lows pattern. What structure is this?
   - — Uptrend
   - — Downtrend
   - **✓** Range
   - — Compression breakout
   - *Explanation:* That's a range — neither buyers nor sellers have taken clear control, so price oscillates between a repeated ceiling and floor.

### Lesson 1.9: Support & Resistance

**Key levels, defined**  
A support level is a floor price has bounced off more than once. A resistance level is a ceiling price has been rejected from more than once. These are the levels every confluence setup in Course 2 is built around.  
*[Illustration: `structure-levels.svg`]*

**Support in context**  
Support usually shows up after a decline — price falls toward a level and buyers step back in, at least twice, before you can call it a real floor.  
*[Illustration: `chart-support.svg`]*

**Resistance in context**  
Resistance usually shows up after an advance — price rises toward a level and sellers step back in, at least twice, before you can call it a real ceiling.  
*[Illustration: `chart-resistance.svg`]*

   - — The same thing as a support level

### Lesson 2.1: What Is a Confluence

**One signal is never enough**  
A confluence is two or more independent signals agreeing on direction at the same time and place. A candlestick pattern alone, or a level alone, is not a trade — TradeWise never scores a single signal as sufficient.  
*[Illustration: `confluence-intro.svg`]*

**Why this matters**  
Markets are noisy. Any one signal produces false positives constantly. Requiring agreement between independent signals is what filters noise from a genuine edge.  
*[Illustration: `confluence-noise.svg`]*

**The 5 core setups, at a glance**  
Across this course and the Advanced course you'll learn 5 confluence setups: Key Level + Candlestick, Trend + Fibonacci Retracement, Structure Break + Retest, Multi-Timeframe Confluence, and Compression Breakout. This course covers the first three.  
*[Illustration: `confluence-five.svg`]*

**What separates a valid setup from a trap**  
A trap looks like a setup at first glance but is missing a component, or one component actively contradicts another. Spotting traps is as much a skill as spotting valid setups — Lesson 5 is built entirely around it.  
*[Illustration: `confluence-trap-intro.svg`]*

**End-of-lesson quiz** (2/3 to pass, unlimited retries):

   - — Any single candlestick pattern
   - **✓** Two or more independent signals agreeing on direction
   - — A type of chart timeframe

   - *Explanation:* A confluence requires two or more independent signals pointing the same direction — never just one.

   - **✓** Because a single signal produces too many false positives on its own
   - — Because the app is designed to reduce how often you trade for its own sake

   - — It doesn't — one clean signal is always enough
   - *Explanation:* Any one signal alone is noisy and unreliable. Requiring agreement between independent signals filters out much of that noise.

### Lesson 2.2: Setup 1: Key Level + Candlestick Confirmation

**The two components**  
Component A: price is at a key level (support or resistance) with at least 2 prior touches. Component B: a reversal candlestick forms right at that level — bullish engulfing, bearish engulfing, pin bar, hammer, or shooting star.  
*[Illustration: `setup1-components.svg`]*

**Valid buy / valid sell**  
Valid BUY: a touch of support plus a bullish engulfing or pin bar at the level. Valid SELL: a touch of resistance plus a bearish engulfing or shooting star at the level.  
*[Illustration: `setup1-valid.svg`]*

**The trap version**  
Price approaches the level but the confirming candle is a doji or has an ambiguous body — no clean directional signal. The correct call is No Trade, not a guess.  
*[Illustration: `setup1-trap.svg`]*

**End-of-lesson quiz** (2/3 to pass, unlimited retries):

1. What are the two components of Setup 1?
   - **✓** A key level and a reversal candlestick at that level
   - — Two moving averages crossing
   - — High volume and a news event
   - — A Fibonacci level and RSI reading
   - *Explanation:* Setup 1 is a key level (support/resistance with 2+ prior touches) plus a reversal candlestick confirming right at that level.

2. Price touches resistance and a bearish engulfing candle forms right at the level. What's the valid call?
   - — Buy
   - **✓** Sell
   - — No Trade — needs a third signal
   - — Wait for a doji
   - *Explanation:* Resistance + bearish engulfing at the level is exactly the valid SELL version of Setup 1.

3. Price touches support but the candle at the level is a doji. What's the correct call?
   - — Buy — support always holds
   - — Sell — doji means reversal
   - **✓** No Trade — no clean confirming candle
   - — Buy with a wider stop
   - *Explanation:* A doji shows indecision, not a confirming reversal candle. Without a clean Component B, this is the trap version — No Trade is correct.

### Lesson 2.3: Setup 2: Trend Continuation + Fibonacci Retracement

*[Illustration: `setup2-valid.svg`]*

*[Illustration: `setup2-trap.svg`]*

### Lesson 2.4: Setup 3: Market Structure Break & Retest

**The components**  
Component A: a clear break of a prior swing high (for a buy) or swing low (for a sell) on the 5M chart, closing beyond it by 10+ pips. Component B: price returns to retest the broken level. Component C: a candlestick confirmation at the retest.  
*[Illustration: `setup3-components.svg`]*

**Valid buy / valid sell**

*[Illustration: `setup3-valid.svg`]*

**The trap version — false break**  
Price breaks the level, but on the 'retest' it aggressively pushes straight back through and continues past — that's not a retest, it's a false break (liquidity sweep). Correct call: No Trade.

### Lesson 3.1: Stop Loss & Take Profit

**Stop Loss: non-negotiable**

A Take Profit (TP) is the price at which you exit having been right. Setting it in advance — before you see the outcome — is what keeps a plan a plan instead of an emotional decision made mid-trade.  
*[Illustration: `risk-tp.svg`]*

**Why both are set before the chart plays**  
TradeWise's core mechanic locks in SL and TP before the outcome plays out — exactly like real trading, where you don't get to change your exit after seeing what happens next.  
*[Illustration: `risk-prehoc.svg`]*

### Lesson 3.2: Lot Size & Pip Value

### Lesson 3.3: The 1% Risk Rule

**The rule itself**  
Risk no more than 1% of your Practice Capital on any single trade. On a $500 account, that's $5 maximum risk per trade — regardless of how confident you feel.  
*[Illustration: `risk-1pct.svg`]*

**Worked example**  
$500 account. 1% risk = $5. Your Stop Loss is 50 pips away. At $0.10/pip (0.01 lots), 50 pips = $5 risked. That's your maximum lot size for this trade: 0.01 lots.  
*[Illustration: `risk-worked-example.svg`]*

**Why 1% instead of 'whatever feels right'**  
At 1% risk, it takes many consecutive losses in a row to meaningfully damage your account — giving your edge time to play out. At 10% risk per trade, a short losing streak can wipe you out before your edge ever gets a fair test.  
*[Illustration: `risk-why1pct.svg`]*

**End-of-lesson quiz** (2/3 to pass, unlimited retries):

### Lesson 3.4: Margin & Leverage

**What leverage means**  
Leverage lets you control a larger position than your capital alone would allow. It amplifies outcomes in both directions — the same 50-pip move produces a bigger dollar swing at higher leverage.  
*[Illustration: `risk-leverage.svg`]*

**What margin actually is**  
Margin is collateral held against your open position, not a fee charged to you. It's returned when the position closes — it just can't be used for anything else while the trade is open.  
*[Illustration: `risk-margin.svg`]*

**Why TradeWise starts at 1:10**  
Low leverage means smaller amplification while you're still building the habit of correct position sizing. Higher leverage tiers unlock later, gated by TradePoints earned through disciplined play — not by account balance alone.  
*[Illustration: `risk-why-1-10.svg`]*

**What a margin call is**  
A margin call happens when losses eat too far into your available capital to support open positions — the broker (or in TradeWise, the simulation) starts closing things down to protect what's left. Correct position sizing is how you avoid ever seeing one.  
*[Illustration: `risk-margincall.svg`]*

   - — Removes the need for a Stop Loss
   - — Only applies to Stock trading
   - *Explanation:* Leverage lets you control a bigger position with the same capital, which amplifies both wins and losses proportionally.

2. What is margin?
   - — A fee taken from every trade
   - **✓** Collateral held against an open position, returned when it closes
   - — A bonus for high-leverage trades
   - — Another word for Stop Loss
   - *Explanation:* Margin is collateral, not a cost — it's tied up while the position is open and released when it closes.

   - — Higher leverage isn't technically possible
   - **✓** Lower leverage limits amplification while the habit of correct sizing is still being built
   - — 1:10 is a legal maximum everywhere
   - — It has nothing to do with learning — it's arbitrary
   - *Explanation:* Starting low keeps early mistakes small while position-sizing discipline is still developing. Higher leverage unlocks later, earned through TradePoints.

### Lesson 5.1: Setup 4: Multi-Timeframe Confluence

### Lesson 5.2: Setup 5: Compression Breakout

**The components**  
Component A: a narrowing range — lower highs and higher lows — visible over 10+ candles on the 5M chart. Component B: a breakout candle closing outside the compression with a body at least 1.5× the average body size of the compression period. Component C: the breakout direction aligns with the 15M trend or momentum.  
*[Illustration: `setup5-components.svg`]*

**Valid buy / valid sell**  
Valid BUY: compression, then a bullish breakout candle closing above the range, with the 15M trend up or neutral. Valid SELL: compression, then a bearish breakout closing below the range, with the 15M confirming.  
*[Illustration: `setup5-valid.svg`]*

**The trap version**  
A breakout wick pokes outside the range but the candle doesn't close beyond it — or the breakout fires directly against a strong 1H trend. Both are correctly No Trade.  
*[Illustration: `setup5-trap.svg`]*

**End-of-lesson quiz** (2/3 to pass, unlimited retries):

1. What defines the 'compression' in Compression Breakout?
   - — A single large candle
   - **✓** A narrowing range — lower highs and higher lows — over 10+ candles
   - — Any sideways price action, regardless of shape
   - — A gap between two candles
   - *Explanation:* Compression specifically means the range is narrowing candle over candle — lower highs meeting higher lows, squeezing toward a point.

2. What size does the breakout candle's body need to be, relative to the compression period?
   - — Any size at all
   - **✓** At least 1.5× the average body size of the compression candles
   - — Exactly the same size as the previous candle
   - — Smaller than the compression candles
   - *Explanation:* The breakout needs real conviction behind it — a body at least 1.5× the compression period's average, not just a marginal push outside the range.

   - — Always trade against the wick
   - — Increase lot size since volatility is rising
   - *Explanation:* Component B requires a close outside the range, not just a wick. Without that close, it's the trap version of Setup 5.

### Lesson 5.3: Advanced Trap Library

**False break / liquidity sweep, revisited**  
At this level, false breaks get sharper: price can sweep through a level aggressively — designed to trigger stop losses clustered just beyond it — before reversing hard the other way.  
*[Illustration: `advtrap-liquiditysweep.svg`]*

**Counter-trend traps**  
A setup with every local component present, but positioned directly against a strong, well-established higher-timeframe trend. These are the most convincing traps precisely because the local pattern is genuinely clean.  
*[Illustration: `advtrap-countertrend.svg`]*

**News-simulation spikes**  
A sharp, fast directional move with no structural basis — no level, no break, no compression. It's volatility without a story. Trading it as if it were a valid setup is a Stage 3+ trap.  
*[Illustration: `advtrap-newsspike.svg`]*

**End-of-lesson quiz** (2/3 to pass, unlimited retries):

1. What is a liquidity sweep?
   - — A slow, gradual price drift
   - **✓** An aggressive push through a level — often to trigger clustered stop losses — before reversing
   - — A type of candlestick pattern
   - — A guaranteed reversal signal
   - *Explanation:* A liquidity sweep is a sharp move through a level, frequently understood as clearing out stop losses sitting just beyond it, before price reverses.

2. Why are counter-trend traps described as 'the most convincing'?
   - — Because they never actually look valid
   - **✓** Because the local pattern genuinely is clean — it's only the higher-timeframe context that makes it a trap
   - — Because they only occur on weekends
   - — Because they always involve a doji
   - *Explanation:* The local setup can be textbook-correct in isolation — what makes it a trap is fighting the higher-timeframe trend, which is easy to miss if you only look at the immediate chart.

3. What structurally distinguishes a news-simulation spike from a valid Setup 5 breakout?
   - — Nothing — they look identical
   - **✓** The spike has no compression, level, or structural basis behind the move

   - — It always aligns with the 15M trend
   - *Explanation:* A valid breakout has a structural setup behind it (compression). A news-spike is volatility with no such basis — trading it as a setup is the trap.

### Lesson 5.4: Session Risk Budgeting & Expectancy

**Beyond per-trade risk: the session budget**  
The 1% rule caps a single trade. A session risk budget caps how much you're willing to risk across an entire sitting — a warning triggers if any single trade exceeds 2% of Practice Capital, even though it's not blocked outright.  
*[Illustration: `advrisk-sessionbudget.svg`]*

**What an R-multiple is**  
An R-multiple expresses a trade's result relative to what you risked. Risk $5 (1R) and make $10, that's a +2R trade. Risk $5 and lose it, that's -1R. Thinking in R lets you compare trades of different sizes fairly.  
*[Illustration: `advrisk-rmultiple.svg`]*

**Expectancy, in one line**  
Expectancy = (win rate × average R won) − (loss rate × average R lost). A positive expectancy system can still have more losing trades than winning ones and be profitable overall — this is why a single loss (or win) never tells the whole story.  
*[Illustration: `advrisk-expectancy.svg`]*

**End-of-lesson quiz** (2/3 to pass, unlimited retries):

1. What does a session risk budget add on top of the 1% per-trade rule?
   - — Nothing — they're the same thing
   - **✓** A ceiling on total risk taken across an entire session, not just one trade
   - — A guarantee against any losses
   - — A higher leverage allowance
   - *Explanation:* The 1% rule governs one trade; the session budget governs the cumulative risk across everything you do in one sitting.

2. You risk $5 (1R) and the trade closes at +$15 profit. What R-multiple is that?
   - — +1R
   - — +2R
   - **✓** +3R
   - — -1R
   - *Explanation:* $15 profit against $5 risked is 3× the risk — a +3R result.

3. Can a trading system with more losing trades than winning trades still be profitable overall?
   - — No — win rate above 50% is always required
   - **✓** Yes — if the average size of wins (in R) sufficiently outweighs the average size of losses
   - — Only if leverage is very high
   - — Only in Arcade Mode
   - *Explanation:* Expectancy depends on win rate AND average R per win/loss together — a system that wins less often but wins bigger can still have positive expectancy overall.

### Lesson 5.5: Intro to Strategy Frameworks

**Why frameworks, not just setups**  
The 5 core setups are individual patterns. A strategy framework is a broader lens for reading the market that several setups can sit inside — this lesson is a primer, not the full depth (see the Strategies Pack for that).  
*[Illustration: `framework-why.svg`]*

**ICT-inspired concepts**  
A family of ideas centred on liquidity — where clusters of stop losses likely sit, and how price is drawn toward them before reversing. You've already seen a piece of this in the liquidity sweep trap.  
*[Illustration: `framework-ict.svg`]*

**SMC (Smart Money Concepts) structure**  
A framework built around identifying where larger participants are likely positioned, using structure breaks and order-flow-style reasoning rather than indicators.  
*[Illustration: `framework-smc.svg`]*

**Momentum continuation**  
A framework focused on trading in the direction of established momentum rather than looking for reversals — the opposite instinct to trading every level as a turning point.  
*[Illustration: `framework-momentum.svg`]*

**End-of-lesson quiz** (2/3 to pass, unlimited retries):

   - — A leverage tier
   - *Explanation:* A framework is a bigger-picture approach to reading the market — setups are specific patterns; a framework is the lens you view many patterns through.

   - *Explanation:* Where Setup 1 looks for reversals at a level, momentum continuation looks the other way — trading with an already-established directional move.
